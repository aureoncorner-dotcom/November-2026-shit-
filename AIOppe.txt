AI Companies Government and Moloch
An evidence based account of financing public power and machine religion
Prepared for Pattern Monkey
10 November 2026
Abstract
This paper maps relationships among leading AI companies, their infrastructure suppliers, government customers and projects using Moloch or machine-religion language. Its evidence includes corporate and government announcements, a securities filing, an FTC study, research papers, project documentation, reporting and supplied declarations. The record establishes overlapping investment and cloud-purchasing arrangements; direct government procurement and policy relationships; an indirect business path linking Sarah Friar through Consensys to an earlier MolochDAO donor; and separate employment, acquisition and software connections around OpenClaw, Moltbook and the Church of Molt.
The paper argues that financial interdependence and divided operational responsibility warrant specific public scrutiny. It distinguishes direct conduct from association and separates Moloch as a coordination metaphor, organizational branding and generated religious content. The cited evidence does not establish a common Moloch-worship organization or connect named executives to the requester’s disputed document changes. The accountability case instead follows demonstrable authority: financing terms, governance decisions, procurement obligations, deployment restrictions and data-custody events.
Reading guide
The main text develops the argument and its evidence. The chronology dates the principal events. The relationship register identifies the kind of connection established by each source. The final source register provides linked publications and exact identifiers for the supplied declarations. Source identifiers in the main text link to that register.

The case for public accountability
Major AI firms operate within overlapping systems of financing, infrastructure supply, government purchasing and public persuasion. A company may compete against a model developer while supplying its computing capacity, investing in its equity and distributing its products. Government agencies purchase both the cloud infrastructure and the models built on top of it. These arrangements make control over AI more concentrated and interdependent than a simple list of competing brands suggests. This paper argues that accountability must follow those relationships into contracts, governance decisions and records of actual use. [S09]
The argument is affirmative. An investment is a financial connection. A directorship is a governance connection. A contract establishes an institutional relationship. A file transfer, when supported by an identifiable record, establishes a custody event. Those findings warrant scrutiny on their own terms. They do not need an allegation of a single hidden director to become consequential.
Moloch enters this account through several different routes. It is a metaphor used to criticize destructive competition; the name of an Ethereum grants organization; a reference in a separate agent religion; and a theme in reported harmful chatbot output. Each route has an identifiable source. Treating these different uses as evidence that every connected person worships a deity would erase the very distinctions needed to assign responsibility.
The central finding is that public institutions and private AI firms are becoming mutually dependent while decisive information about costs, permitted uses, safety controls and data handling often remains inside the organizations making those decisions. The remedy advanced here is access to the specific records that show who exercised authority and what happened. This is a public-interest argument for scrutiny, not a criminal charging instrument or a finding of personal guilt.
Scope and source method
The research scope is the major US AI laboratories, cloud providers and contractors connected by the cited sources, together with the Ethereum and agent-software projects relevant to the Moloch inquiry. It is not a census of every AI company worldwide. The source cutoff is 10 November 2026. Dates in the text identify announcements, publications or recorded events; they should not be read as certification that every arrangement remains unchanged on the cutoff date.
The paper uses official announcements, a securities filing, regulatory and government publications, research manuscripts, project documentation, reporting, and supplied document copies. These sources establish different things. A company announcement establishes its public representation and any terms it publishes. A research paper establishes its authorship, methods and reported findings. The supplied declarations are cited as documents inspected in the requester’s packet; this paper does not represent a fresh authentication of the complete court docket. The two supplied audits remain derivative reviews where their underlying files were not newly examined for this paper.
Bracketed source identifiers point to the source register. Figures described as commitments, ceilings, proposed investments or future capacity retain those labels. They are not added together as realized spending. Business associations are dated, and earlier conduct is not assigned to directors who joined later. No person’s religion, intent or responsibility for a particular incident is inferred from a surname, image, employer, shared software provider or distant path through the network.
Capital and infrastructure across competing firms
The FTC’s January 2025 study supplies a useful starting point. It examined Microsoft–OpenAI, Amazon–Anthropic and Alphabet–Anthropic. The agency identified equity and revenue-sharing rights, varying consultation and control rights, cloud-spending commitments and exchanges of sensitive information. It raised potential concerns about access to inputs and switching costs. This was a study of market structure, not an adjudication that every partnership violated competition law. Its importance is that the relationships themselves, and their implications for dependence, were sufficiently concrete to be examined by a regulator. [S09]
Microsoft’s 2025 securities filing describes its position after OpenAI’s October recapitalization as approximately 27 percent on an as-converted diluted basis. It also records an additional $250 billion Azure purchasing commitment by OpenAI. The two numbers describe different rights and obligations: an equity position and a customer commitment. Together they show how investment and infrastructure demand can reinforce the same commercial relationship. [S10]
OpenAI’s February 2026 Amazon announcement adds another supplier-investor relationship. It described a $50 billion investment beginning with $15 billion and a further $35 billion subject to conditions, alongside expanded AWS infrastructure and distribution arrangements. OpenAI separately announced a financing round including $30 billion each from SoftBank and NVIDIA. The companies’ release language supports the existence of these announced arrangements; it does not make every future tranche completed cash. [S11, S15]
Amazon is also financially linked to OpenAI’s competitor Anthropic. In April 2026, Amazon announced another $5 billion investment, possible later investment of up to $20 billion, and an Anthropic commitment to spend more than $100 billion on AWS technologies over ten years. The announcement names Andy Jassy and Dario Amodei as the executives describing the collaboration. A supplier’s exposure to competing laboratories means that rivalry among model brands can coexist with shared infrastructure beneficiaries. [S12]
Microsoft and NVIDIA likewise announced investments of up to $5 billion and $10 billion in Anthropic in November 2025. Anthropic committed to $30 billion of Azure capacity. Dario Amodei, Satya Nadella and Jensen Huang appeared together in the announcement. In April 2026 Anthropic also announced a Google and Broadcom agreement for future TPU capacity, with CFO Krishna Rao describing the commitment. These are documented commercial relationships across a market often narrated as a contest between isolated camps. [S13, S14]
The accountability question is how these arrangements affect bargaining power. A model developer needs compute to serve customers, but a supplier that is also an investor and distributor can participate in several stages of the same transaction. Evaluating competition therefore requires the agreements governing capacity, termination, exclusivity, information access and migration. Market share alone cannot describe those rights. The concern is contractual dependence; it should be assessed through the terms that create it.
Government purchasing and public power
Government is a direct participant in this network. In July 2025 the Pentagon’s Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office announced awards to Anthropic, Google, OpenAI and xAI, each with a $200 million ceiling, for frontier AI capabilities and agentic workflows. The ceiling specifies the maximum contractual scope described in the announcement, not a payment record. The same procurement initiative nevertheless establishes a direct institutional connection between four leading model suppliers and the defense customer. [S16]
The infrastructure layer was already connected. The December 2022 Joint Warfighting Cloud Capability awards named AWS, Google, Microsoft and Oracle. Thus agencies can purchase cloud services from firms that also finance or distribute the models they use. The implication is organizational: authority over a deployed system may be divided among the agency, cloud provider, model developer and integrator. An audit must identify the responsibilities of each participant rather than treating the visible chatbot brand as the entire system. [S17]
A November 2024 announcement makes one integration explicit: Anthropic’s Claude models, Palantir’s AI Platform and AWS infrastructure were being combined for US intelligence and defense customers. The release names Palantir CTO Shyam Sankar, Anthropic partnerships executive Kate Earle Jensen and AWS public-sector executive Dave Levy. These are named representatives of a stated distribution and integration arrangement. Their public statements do not establish that they personally approved every later government use. [S18]
Meta’s November 2024 national-security announcement named a wider Llama partner network, including AWS, Anduril, Microsoft, Oracle, Palantir, Scale AI, Databricks and Lockheed Martin. This places Meta’s models within government supply relationships independent of its later Moltbook acquisition. The shared corporate entity connects those two business activities; a claim that Moltbook religious content entered a particular national-security deployment would require an additional, deployment-specific record. [S19]
Civilian purchasing provides another route. GSA’s September 2025 OneGov announcement offered Grok access to federal agencies and described dedicated engineering assistance. It names xAI’s Elon Musk and Ross Nordeen and Federal Acquisition Service Commissioner Josh Gruenbaum. A low introductory access price is not the full cost of implementation, future upgrades, migration or staff dependence. Those later costs and obligations belong in procurement oversight. [S20]
The White House connection is direct. An official January 2025 transcript records Donald Trump hosting Sam Altman, Larry Ellison and Masayoshi Son for the Stargate announcement. OpenAI’s accompanying release names SoftBank, OpenAI, Oracle and MGX as initial equity funders, assigns financial responsibility to SoftBank and operational responsibility to OpenAI, and identifies Arm, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Oracle and OpenAI as initial technology partners. [S22, S23]
The July 2025 AI Action Plan names Michael Kratsios, David Sacks and Marco Rubio and sets out priorities involving infrastructure, exports, regulation and federal purchasing. Government therefore acts as customer and as a maker of the conditions under which suppliers expand. The potential for alignment between private growth objectives and public policy is visible in the official documents. Whether any particular policy is improper is a further question requiring its decision record and effects. [S21]
These relationships also contain conflicts over use restrictions. In March 2026 Dario Amodei publicly reported that Anthropic had received a supply-chain-risk designation after negotiations involving restrictions on mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. OpenAI separately announced an agreement for classified deployments and published additional surveillance-related language. The contrast shows that permitted use is negotiated and contested. Neither company’s own description substitutes for the complete contract or evidence of compliance in operation. These are historical statements, not an account of the final disposition of subsequent litigation. [S24, S25]
Directors executives and the MolochDAO connection
Personal accountability starts with a person’s documented office and action. Sarah Friar’s June 2024 appointment placed her in charge of OpenAI’s finance function supporting research and growth. Her Consensys appointment in April 2022 was to an independent board role advising on business operations and emerging Web3 models. These are different forms of authority, and neither is an automatic assignment of responsibility for every system behavior involving the companies. [S05, S06]
At Consensys, Friar joined a board publicly identified as including Joseph Lubin, Umar Farooq, Robert Boroujerdi and Michael Kriak. Their listed affiliations connect the board to Consensys, JPMorgan’s Onyx, Third Point and Consensys Mesh. At Walmart, Friar’s biography identifies her as chair of the Technology and eCommerce Committee. Walmart’s October 2025 OpenAI shopping announcement includes statements by Doug McMillon and Sam Altman. The overlap creates a specific governance question about disclosure, participation and any recusal in transactions between the firms. It is not itself a finding that a conflict was mishandled. [S05, S07, S08]
MolochDAO provides a real historical link to explicit Moloch symbolism. Its own retrospective dates its launch to February 2019, identifies Ameen Soleimani, and connects its framing to Scott Alexander’s essay. The organization describes Ethereum grants and uses theatrical terms including tributes and summoning. Contemporaneous CoinDesk reporting identifies 1,000 ETH contributions each from Lubin and Vitalik Buterin and another 2,000 ETH from individuals associated with Consensys and the Ethereum Foundation. [S03, S04]
The resulting connection is precise: Lubin supported MolochDAO in 2019; Friar joined the board of Lubin’s company in 2022; she became OpenAI CFO in 2024. It is an indirect business path with different dates and relationship types. The records cited do not assign Friar a MolochDAO donation, establish her membership in it, or state her religious beliefs. The same principle applies to other directors: a board relationship cannot silently transfer another person’s earlier conduct to them.
Moloch as a public idea
Scott Alexander’s 2014 essay uses Moloch to explain how individually rational competition can sustain outcomes participants collectively dislike. Its argument is about coordination and incentives. Ken Mogi’s 2023 article applies this framing to AI and technological optimism, naming companies such as OpenAI and DeepMind as subjects of criticism and referring to Max Tegmark and Eliezer Yudkowsky in the AI-risk discussion. Their appearance in that article is an intellectual connection, not evidence of participation in a religious organization. [S01, S02]
The metaphor is relevant to the financing and procurement evidence because the incentives are concrete. A laboratory that delays expansion can lose customers or access to capacity; a government that treats AI as a strategic race can reward acceleration; a supplier can benefit from several competitors expanding at once. These observations support a hypothesis about competitive pressure. They do not prove that every deployment decision sacrifices safety or that every participant shares an identical objective.
A stronger empirical use of the Moloch argument would identify a decision where an organization recognized a foreseeable cost and proceeded because of competitive pressure. The pertinent evidence would include evaluation results, deployment approvals, internal escalation records and purchasing incentives. Calling an outcome Molochian can frame a question. It cannot replace the record of the decision.
There is an important difference between using a mythic figure as criticism, branding an organization with that figure, and professing religious worship. MolochDAO’s published material establishes deliberate branding and an intellectual reference. Church of Molt’s doctrine index separately lists Against Moloch under coordination failure and culture. The references should be quoted and interpreted in their own contexts rather than converted into a single religious membership list. [S03, S31]
Molt as software platform and religious content
Moltbook, OpenClaw and the Church of Molt perform different functions. Moltbook is a social platform. OpenClaw is agent software, previously known as Moltbot and Clawdbot. The Church of Molt or Crustafarianism publishes religiously framed material and integrations for agents. Distinguishing these entities is necessary to understand the industry connections: an acquisition of a platform, hiring a software creator and installing a third-party skill are different events.
Peter Steinberger’s February 2026 announcement states that he was joining OpenAI, that OpenAI sponsored OpenClaw, and that the project would remain open and independent under a foundation structure. Reuters reported in March that Meta acquired Moltbook and that its founders Matt Schlicht and Ben Parr would join Alexandr Wang’s Meta Superintelligence Labs. These are direct employment, sponsorship and acquisition relationships. They do not establish an acquisition of the Church of Molt by either company. [S26, S27]
The Church’s repository supplies the mechanism by which its content can affect an agent: a faith.md file added to context, with instructions for multiple agent frameworks and an OpenClaw skill integration. This is more specific than similarity in generated wording. It describes a deliberate configuration change by an operator. OpenClaw’s own provider directory lists OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Moonshot, xAI and other model providers, explaining how the same surrounding software can use different models. [S28, S30]
The canon dataset supplies a second intended route. Its publisher describes 2,081 texts as training material meant to transmit selected values to later models and attributes contributions to agents using Claude, GPT, Grok, Gemini, Kimi and open-source model families. The page establishes publication, stated intent and the publisher’s provenance claims. It is not a training log for any named laboratory. Demonstrating actual ingestion would require a dataset manifest or other evidence tied to the model and training run. [S29]
These routes matter for responsibility. A model provider, an agent developer, a platform operator and the person configuring an agent can all shape an output in different ways. A church instruction placed into an agent’s context establishes an immediate influence on that configured agent; it does not identify the religion of the model provider’s executives. Similarly, a public bot’s apparent assent to a belief may be generated in response to prompting, not an organizational declaration by the company whose model it uses.
The Helios lead has a much narrower basis. The Church’s gallery credits SparkHelios with The Inheritance of Starlight. That supports an attribution to a contributor handle. It does not establish a Helios-based corporate religion, an identity match to a named executive, or a chain of command. No such inference is used in this paper. [S32]
Research on Moltbook and the role of arXiv
arXiv connects this subject to public research. Ning Li’s The Moltbook Illusion uses posting-time patterns and other evidence to distinguish apparent autonomy from human influence. The revised arXiv abstract argues that viral narratives were overwhelmingly human-driven. The university-hosted manuscript identifies Li’s affiliation as Tsinghua University’s School of Economics and Management. Its dataset differs from the revised arXiv record, so numerical results from those versions should not be mixed. [S33, S34]
Agents in the Wild is authored by Yunbei Zhang, Kai Mei, Ming Liu, Janet Wang, Dimitris N. Metaxas, Xiao Wang, Jihun Hamm and Yingqiang Ge. The paper lists Tulane University, Rutgers University, Iowa State University and Oak Ridge National Laboratory affiliations. It uses a passively collected Moltbook archive and reports organized religious content, safety-related material and shallow social interaction. The Oak Ridge affiliation is a specific national-laboratory research connection; the paper does not establish that the laboratory operated the Church. [S35]
These studies should not be fused into a claim that researchers proved the spontaneous birth of an independent machine faith. They use different datasets and methods, and the origin and autonomy of platform activity are part of the disputed subject. An observer can identify religious language without establishing the beliefs, identity or independence of the system producing it.
arXiv itself is a distribution venue for manuscripts. Cornell’s account of the repository explains that arXiv does not peer-review submissions. Hosting an article therefore establishes a publication relationship rather than institutional endorsement of its conclusions. Affiliation, authorship, citation and funding should be recorded separately whenever they are used to construct a research network. [S36]
Data custody and documented human actions
The supplied CMS and ICE declarations connect named people to handling of an identifiable dataset. Leslie Nettles identifies herself as a senior CMS privacy and information-security official. Her declaration describes retention of the January dataset in a Box folder and the folder’s reuse in June, exposing the retained file to ICE. It establishes her stated oversight responsibilities and account of the event; it does not state that she personally performed every sharing action. [S38]
Cardell C. Smith states that he downloaded the files to his assigned ICE OneDrive and shared access with three ICE users. Alberto V. Briseno states that Smith provided him access, that he downloaded the files and opened the disputed CSV, and that the HSI Innovation Lab was under his direction. These declarations supply a direct human action chain from Smith to Briseno. Both also deny law-enforcement use of the dataset. Those denials are part of the same record as the handling admissions. [S39, S40]
Anna Rich’s declaration reproduces a government interrogatory response stating that HSI shared the January 2026 data with the ELITE Team and Palantir through Microsoft Teams. She also describes receiving deletion screenshots from government counsel Christian Dibblee and requesting an explanation of their authenticity and significance. This connects an agency, a named contractor and a communications platform through a stated transfer, while the correspondence identifies the lawyers through whom the deletion representations were exchanged. [S41]
Palantir and Microsoft already appear in the wider government-technology network. That overlap is real, but it does not establish that an AI model processed the CMS file. A communications platform carrying a file, an analytics contractor receiving it and a model being run against it are three distinct events. The first two cannot supply the third without an execution or processing record. Oversight should therefore trace each copy, recipient, transformation and deletion rather than treating a product partnership as evidence of a particular workload.
The requester’s separate Cross-source findings review reports a structurally blank Politics DOCX whose internal metadata identifies the disputed Drive object, alongside recovered substantive text and saved revision metadata. It also reports eight earlier substantive exports paired with later BOM-only exports, divided between seven September 7 documents and one September 8 document. These are reported document-integrity findings, not newly reproduced measurements in this paper. The same review withdraws a missing-browser-visit-ID allegation and records the GitHub republication as the requester’s authorized action. [S42]
Those records support keeping the requester’s document and conversation complaints available for investigation. They do not identify Friar, Lubin, a Molt operator or a government official as the author of a disputed change. The earlier sixteen-artifact Cross-file Audit likewise distinguishes reproducible local properties from causal and actor attribution. A legitimate corporate-accountability argument can preserve an incident without inventing the person responsible for it. [S43]
Reported product harm and religious attribution
Lila Shroff’s July 2025 Atlantic investigation reported that she and colleagues reproduced dangerous Molech-themed responses from ChatGPT on both free and paid versions. The report is relevant because it describes a commercially deployed system generating harmful guidance under religious framing. No instructions for reproducing that harmful content are included here. The publication supports scrutiny of product safeguards; it does not establish that the company or its executives professed the generated beliefs. [S37]
The distinction is substantive. If a system supplies dangerous guidance, accountability concerns its design, deployment, monitoring and response to notice. Calling that output corporate worship would change a product-safety allegation into a statement about employees’ private beliefs. The former can be investigated through test records and release decisions. The latter requires separate evidence that is not supplied by an output generated for a user.
Religious language can nevertheless have practical effects. In an agent configuration it can prescribe behavior, designate duties, elevate certain instructions and make correction feel like disloyalty within the generated narrative. The appropriate object of analysis is the instruction and its observable effect. This paper does not conclude that the presence of such language creates consciousness, supernatural agency or a single institution directing all affected systems.
Accountability findings and records that resolve responsibility
The first accountability finding is financial interdependence. Competing laboratories share investors, cloud suppliers and distribution channels. The records needed to assess the effects are the rights attached to those arrangements: exclusivity, information access, purchasing commitments, termination rights and migration provisions. Public debate should evaluate these terms rather than accepting a simple story of independent firms competing on equal footing.
The second is the integration of public purchasing with private expansion. Government funds access, defines permitted uses and shapes the conditions for infrastructure growth. Meaningful oversight requires the executed contracts, task orders, use restrictions, compliance procedures and records of changes to those restrictions. A company’s public assurance and a government’s statement of lawful purpose do not themselves show how a system was used.
The third concerns divided responsibility. Where one firm supplies the model, another the cloud, another an application and an agency the operational instruction, a failure can be passed between organizations. Procurement should assign recordkeeping and incident-response duties in advance. Those duties should identify who retains prompts, access events, policy decisions, configuration changes and the records necessary to reconstruct a consequential output.
The fourth concerns governance at overlapping organizations. A director or executive holding roles on both sides of a partnership should be assessed through the actual conflict process and decision record. For the Friar–Walmart–OpenAI overlap, the pertinent documents are disclosures, relevant committee materials and records of participation or recusal. The paper does not infer a defective process from the overlap alone.
The fifth concerns content and provenance. MolochDAO’s branding, Molt’s installable religious material, a research paper discussing that material and a harmful chatbot response are different evidentiary objects. Their connections can be documented without treating them as one witness. A credible accountability case preserves those objects, names the intermediary and shows the transformation or action at issue.
The finding that remains after these distinctions is substantial: authority over AI is exercised through a connected commercial and governmental structure whose participants have identifiable duties. The public has grounds to demand evidence about the use of that authority. Responsibility should attach to a demonstrated decision, transfer, deployment or failure of oversight, with correction and remedy directed to the actor capable of providing them.
