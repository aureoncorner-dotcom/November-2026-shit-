**The All-Clear Was Wrong**

ICE said the data had been deleted and no other sources remained in its holdings. Its own later account described copies it had missed.

The assurance came before the work was complete.

In his July 8, 2026 declaration, ICE official Alberto Briseno recounts his earlier belief that deletion was finished. He then describes a copy found in AWS storage and further discoveries through Microsoft 365 searches. Logs showed that six users had had copies; the file remained in two users’ storage. Changed filenames and altered contents, he acknowledged, made it difficult to locate every version. The agency’s own sworn chronology establishes the gap between what it had assured the court and what it had actually accounted for. [Fourth Briseno declaration, ¶¶6–24](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.452203/gov.uscourts.cand.452203.181.1.pdf)

The same declaration describes corrective searches and deletions and denies operational use of the dataset. Those facts belong in the account. Corrective work deserves credit. It cannot make the earlier assurance accurate after the fact. [Fourth Briseno declaration, ¶¶17–24](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.452203/gov.uscourts.cand.452203.181.1.pdf)

The trail also reaches a named contractor. A government response reproduced by California Deputy Attorney General Anna Rich states that the January 7 data were shared with the ELITE Team and Palantir through Microsoft Teams, then deleted from the chat. That acknowledgment establishes a specific transfer. It does not establish a contractor download, operational use, or what happened to every downstream copy. **Deleting the shared item does not, by itself, account for everything that may have followed the sharing.** [Rich declaration, ¶4](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.452203/gov.uscourts.cand.452203.182.1.pdf)

The court had permitted defined categories of sharing while restricting which information and people could be covered. Those boundaries matter. The existence of a transfer alone does not establish a violation; compliance depends on what was selected, shared, and used. [December 29 injunction, pp. 6–7](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.452203/gov.uscourts.cand.452203.148.0.pdf)

None of that makes the accountability demand complicated.

Identify the data and known derivatives. Account for the relevant systems and recipients. Show what was searched, what was found, what was deleted, and what remains uncertain. Explain why the earlier process missed copies. Distinguish data that survived deletion from data transferred again afterward. Show what changed to prevent the same failure.

That is a demand for a checkable account. It requires a defined search, documented methods, and disclosed limits—not impossible certainty about every conceivable copy. If the evidence establishes completed repair, credit it. An earlier failure does not prove that the failure continues today.

But the repair has to reach the records themselves. An instruction starts the work. A deletion performs an action. Verification establishes what that action accomplished. Calling the process complete cannot substitute for completing it.

The government’s own chronology exposed an assurance that went beyond the facts. **Account for the data. State the limits. Show the repair. Completion has to be demonstrated before it is declared.**
