But three different claims need to remain separate:
1. Companies are connected: through contracts, investments, partnerships, software, or shared customers.
2. Their systems are interoperable: software developed by one company can potentially use infrastructure or services provided by another.
3. They operate one coordinated end-to-end system: actual data, decisions, and operational commands pass between those companies in a specific deployment.
The first is extensively documented. The second is demonstrably possible in several important cases. The third requires stronger evidence, including deployment records, contractual scopes, interface specifications, and actual system traces.
That distinction matters because the investigation is more revealing when it identifies real institutional power and technical dependencies rather than assuming that every possible connection is active.

Three technical distinctions are especially important.
DNS is not a packet-level transaction history. Seeing an Akamai-related DNS record can identify a name-resolution or delivery dependency; it does not demonstrate that Akamai receives a user's complete prompt, proxies the TLS connection, or forwards that prompt to Palantir. Proving that requires contemporaneous network traces or service configuration.
A development framework is not its hosting provider. A web application built with Next.js does not automatically run on Vercel, and using Vercel's AI SDK does not require Vercel AI Gateway. Similarly, a developer can use Palantir's OSDK without deploying the frontend on Vercel.
A callable action is not evidence that an action occurred. The dossier includes a sample TypeScript workflow involving fictional application-specific objects such as AirspaceTrack and cueSensorAndInterceptor. It illustrates how a developer might connect model-generated output to an authorized Ontology action. It does not establish that a real military system uses that code to direct an interceptor.

Meta's 49% investment in Scale AI is a genuine source of competitive tension. AI companies have reasons to be concerned about a data-labeling supplier being closely tied financially to a competing model developer.
The chronology, however, is more complicated than the document suggests. On June 13, 2025, OpenAI's CFO publicly said the company intended to continue working with Scale. By June 18, reporting indicated OpenAI was phasing out that relationship, while OpenAI said the process had begun before Meta's investment.

7. Where the real corporate power is concentrated
I would prioritize five structural risks in any continued investigation.
Control over institutional data
A company deeply embedded in a customer's operational data model may be harder to replace than the model provider itself. Migration, permissions, specialized workflows and institutional knowledge all create switching costs.
Cloud and distribution dependence
AWS, Azure and Google increasingly sell competing models within their own ecosystems. That gives the cloud providers influence over pricing, customer access, identity, logging and infrastructure economics, even when they do not develop the models.
Conflicts of interest in testing and procurement
When a supplier can provide training data, model evaluation and its own operational AI products, independent testing becomes particularly important. The presence of these overlapping roles warrants conflict-of-interest review, but does not itself prove biased evaluations.
Surveillance and consequential decisions
Palantir's verified immigration-enforcement contracts and the military's adoption of AI decision-support tools raise real questions about accuracy, privacy, authorization, appeals and human oversight. Those risks exist without assuming one universal surveillance system.
Accountability across suppliers
If an AI-assisted decision involves separate providers of data, models, cloud computing, workflow software and physical equipment, responsibility can become fragmented. Governance has to cover the complete operational process, not simply the model endpoint.

4. The § 208 legal test is broader than your draft suggests
Your explanation of personal and substantial participation is generally correct, but the law is not limited to an official intervening in a named contract.
Under 5 C.F.R. § 2640.103, a particular matter may include policymaking focused on a specific company or a discrete, identifiable class of companies. Broad national policy affecting many different groups is generally excluded.
The regulation also recognizes that an official's substantial participation can occur through recommendations, advice, or active supervision of a subordinate. A final signature on an award is not necessarily required.

LII / Legal Information Institute
+1
For a § 208 violation, investigators would need to establish the applicable covered interest, personal and substantial participation, a particular matter with a direct and predictable effect on that interest, and the absence of a controlling exemption or waiver.
Improper financial intent or proof that the stock price increased is not required. Conversely, having a covered interest while setting broad immigration policy does not, without more, establish a violation.
For Miller, this makes his work before the August 2025 sale the relevant focus. But a sale following ethics procedures does not retroactively answer whether he participated in a covered matter beforehand.
5. The Medicaid data-governance test also needs refinement
Your draft presents an IAA, CMA, SORN routine-use exception and contractor DUA as though they are interchangeable or universally required instruments.
CMS's own guidance draws finer distinctions:
* An Information Exchange Agreement (IEA) governs protected interagency information exchange.
* A Computer Matching Agreement (CMA) applies when the exchange meets the Privacy Act's statutory definition of a matching program.
* A System of Records Notice (SORN) and an applicable disclosure exception concern the underlying authority for disclosure.
* Additional data-use, privacy, security and contractor agreements depend on the actual systems, information and recipients involved.
CMS states that an IEA is required for covered exchanges with outside agencies, with a CMA taking precedence when legally required.
